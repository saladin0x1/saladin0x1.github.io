No Light

How two function hooks defeated a mobile RASP, what the runtime's own cryptography did next, and the raw syscalls that closed it. The work passed review. The passport did not. A case file in nine sections and one gate.

Salah-Eddine Elouali November 22, 2026 ~11 min read

The short version

  • A mobile RASP read every sensor through libc: /proc files via fopen, system properties via __system_property_find, thread enumeration via opendir

  • The fix: raw dual-arch syscalls, filesystem-magic validation, dual-source disagreement. Merged upstream
  • Against the hardened build, the same attack dies at second 29

01Every good rejection starts with a compliment

The internship was at QuartzLab, a European security lab. Also not their name. Nothing in this file is, except the facts and the decoder at the end. Six months of research on fighting dynamic binary instrumentation: catching the tools that watch your code run. The subject I would have paid to be allowed to work on. I applied with a working detector: raw syscalls, dual-source sensors, and honest writeups of everything that didn't fire. It is public, failures included: pinocchio, the repository and the writeup. I named it for the nose. The subject is processes that lie about what they are.

The verdict took three weeks to arrive, and it arrived before the kindness did. The internship is only open to candidates already based in the European Union. The same email apologizes that the requirement had not been clearly stated in the original posting. The posting carried no such gate. The gate arrived with the reply, and the reply apologized for the gap.

The work passed review. The passport did not.

Some context, because the file should say where it happened. I am in the final year of engineering school in Morocco: EMSI, five years of systems and networks, cybersecurity at the end. The Moroccan system puts that year level with an M2, and either label ends the same way, with the internship the degree requires. It is the year you are supposed to stop being a student and start being a researcher. The solid security internships here are scarce. Not nonexistent. Scarce. The serious ones, the kind that trade in real systems instead of coffee runs and certificates, sit mostly across the water. That is the outlet for us: same time zone, same curriculum lineage, labs taught from the same textbooks.

The outlet has a filter bolted to its front door. Not a filter on the work. A filter on the passport. The polite word on the page is residency. The practical word is visa, and the arithmetic never changes: sponsoring a foreign intern is paperwork, signing a local one is a signature. So the filter never reads the code. It reads one field on one document, returns its verdict, and never opens. We are told to be excellent. Then excellence turns out not to be the question being asked.

The applications went out together that October, two days apart: the crystal's first, then the bird's, a Security Engineer Intern seat. The bird answered first, and its answer was a take-home project. Twenty-four hours on the clock, and the brief was better than fair. A security harness that prints ten passes and exits green while the access control underneath is broken: repair it so it can no longer lie. A fictional team, HeatherCheck, reporting green while the door stands open. The brief even invited AI, provided the output gets verified. I should have savored that exercise. The timestamps say I opened the zip at 17:24 and sealed the submission at 18:15. The rejection came within two days: a form letter that said the project had been carefully reviewed, offered no reason, and wished me luck. Two days after sealing the rushed one, verdict already in hand, I rebuilt the whole submission properly, for nobody, because the file deserved it.

So the count stands at two. Rejected by the bird for fifty-one minutes of haste. Rejected by the crystal, weeks later, for a document. Then the bird's product fell to two hooks, the fix went upstream, and the merge was signed by the bird's own CEO. The gate said no. The git log said yes.

The file keeps dates, so here is the week it keeps. Monday: the crystal's verdict arrives at last, residency, the end of that road; I appeal the same afternoon, send them the writeup link myself, and close the appeal on a promise: the repository stays public, in case the team ever finds it useful. Tuesday, 20:50: the sensors in the bird's public repository learn to read through raw syscalls, on a branch with my name on it. Thursday: the bird stages its next release around the fix. Friday, 10:53: the bird's CEO merges the branch. Friday, evening: the bird's advisory desk answers that the project is brand new with no active usage to qualify. One working week, three verdicts, and only the middle one read the code.

And a question the file leaves open: had the bird's door opened in October, would this repository have been my first ticket? Interns get handed the codebase in week one. Outsiders have to take it. The work did not wait to find out which one I was.

One more entry, because the file does not need my help to be strange. Their exercise was about a harness reporting green while access control failed underneath, and the brief called those results symptoms, not verdicts. Weeks later I mapped their product doing exactly that: every sensor reporting green while I answered for every channel. They wrote the exam on untrustworthy green, and shipped the product that still trusted it. I will not claim the left hand knew what the right hand wrote. The file only notes that both hands belong to the same bird.

The kindness arrived after the no, crossed in transit with my appeal, and it was kind. The thinking was good. The ud2/RIP and SMC tests "stood out as more original than what we usually see." Reporting honestly that they did not detect Pin "is the right reflex." And the note I keep in my pocket: "The raw syscalls and heavy parsing add a lot of code without making detection stronger." Fair, for the tracer they test against. A tracer instruments your instructions; it doesn't answer for your libraries. Production is the opposite arrangement. Nobody instruments your instructions, and everybody lies through your libraries.

The thread closes with three sentences worth keeping. "You were a good candidate," and the answer stays no. The EU requirement "isn't something I have any control over": even the man at the gate says he is not the reader of the rule. And the sign-off: keep going with this research. I did. The receipt is a merge commit further down this file.

So when OstrichLab, a mobile RASP of the kind banks ship inside their apps, turned out to read every sensor through libc, the entire product was one interposition layer wide. And the fix, merged upstream by OstrichLab's own CEO, is a raw-syscall reader.

The weight the reviewer would have trimmed is the part that holds.

Keep that email in your pocket. It comes back in the coda.

02The promise

The vendor: open-source core, paid constellation around it, banks on the customer slide. I call them OstrichLab. Not their name either. The bird earns it in the coda.

OstrichLab's whole pitch is AI-powered security. AI agents that log in with one-time codes, challenge TLS pinning, bypass obfuscation, and back every finding with a working exploit. Agentic pentesting that "replaces manual application penetration testing." They even use AI-assisted tests to evaluate their own runtime defenses during development. The models may be excellent. The automation may be real. None of it asked the question I asked, which was: who answers when the detector calls fopen?

The product belongs to a category the industry calls RASP: runtime application self-protection. The app ships with a native runtime stitched inside it. At boot the runtime wakes, looks around, and keeps looking on a randomized schedule. It reads /proc/self/maps for injected libraries. It reads /proc/self/status for a tracer. It walks thread names looking for debug agents. It reads system properties for emulator and root signatures. If it decides the environment is hostile, it refuses to hand over the decryption keys, and the app's protected classes stay ciphertext.

The claim, from the README: a modified environment cannot decrypt your code.

The design philosophy is sound. If you cannot trust your environment, refuse to run. Fail closed, not open. Detection feeds enforcement, enforcement is the last line, and the last line is made of keys.

The philosophy had one assumption in it. It assumed the runtime would be the one asking the questions.

03Reading the instrument

I did not start by attacking the checks. I started by mapping how the runtime knows things. For every security decision it makes, there is a question, and beneath the question there is a channel, and at the bottom of the channel there is a function call.

question channel function is a tracer present? /proc/self/status fopen → read injected libraries? /proc/self/maps fopen → read debug agent alive? thread names opendir → readdir emulator? system properties __system_property_find root? su paths, props fopen / property_find

Every channel ended at libc. And libc lives in the same process as the attacker.

This is the part I want to be careful with, because it is the actual finding. There was no broken check. No missing validation, no logic bug, no parser flaw. Every individual check was fine. The defect was one level up, in the epistemology: the runtime was asking "am I under attack?" through functions its attacker can answer for. It is not a bug in any sensor. It is a property of where the sensors are wired.

The answers were valid. The questions were wrong.

An astrolabe tells you where you stand by reading the sky. This one read the sky through a window, and the attacker was standing outside with a ladder and a very good memory for star charts.

04The touch

The attack is almost embarrassing to describe.

Attach interposers on two functions: fopen and __system_property_find. When the runtime opens a /proc path, redirect the read to a memory file I prepared earlier: same name, same shape, honest-looking content, none of the incriminating lines. When it asks for system properties, answer from a matching table: the device is stock, the boot is pristine, ro.debuggable is whatever the key schedule wants to hear. The thread enumeration was not even necessary to defeat; the names it hunts for were simply never there, because the agent renames its own threads out of politeness.

What the checks saw was a clean world, because the checks were the ones being shown the world. What the key gates saw was a stock device, because the gates read the same channel. And the runtime's cryptography did what cryptography does: it verified. Not the device. The forgery.

Then it did the quietest, most generous thing in this whole file. It derived the keys, decrypted the protected classes, and passed them to the class loader. The protection opened the vault, verified my forged credentials, and helped me carry the bags out.

The app became a weapon against itself. Its sensors were the attack surface. Its crypto was the delivery mechanism. Its fail-closed design was the only thing standing between me and the plaintext, and it closed on nothing.

05The tape

A touch nobody scores is just a claim. So I recorded it.

ConditionResult
Clean launch, no instrumentationAlive; all checks pass; decrypts normally
Fully instrumented, sensors interposedAlive past 110 s; all check groups green; protected classes decrypted; payload extracted to disk
Same attack, hardened buildProcess killed at second 29

The extracted payload sat on disk as a dex while the monitor thread, on its randomized sleep cycle, kept reporting that everything was fine. Detection latency in this product is up to about 90 seconds by design; the sleep intervals are deliberately randomized so an attacker cannot time around them. Respect to the design. It was defending against an attacker who plays by its clock. I did not need to beat the clock. I only needed to be the one the clock asked.

For the record: keys recovered, ciphertext decrypted, output verified against the app's own compiled artifacts. The receipts are timestamps and file hashes.

06The corner adopts the defense

Here is where this file stops being a bypass and starts being something else.

I wrote the fix. The sensor reads moved from libc to raw syscalls, issued through inline assembly on both ARM flavors, straight to the kernel. Filesystem-magic validation on every opened path: if a "proc" file does not sit on procfs, it is not a proc file, and a memfd pretending to be one has the wrong magic number. Reads stream in bounded chunks instead of trusting reported sizes. And where the design allowed it, two independent sources must agree before a verdict counts: a forged answer and an unforged one disagree, and disagreement is itself a signal.

before: sensors → fopen / property_find → verdicts (one interposition point = everything) after: sensors → raw svc syscalls → fs-magic check → dual-source (forged answers must now also forge the filesystem)

The kernel citations went in with the code. Every mechanism claim in the commit trail links to its source: kernel.org for the filesystem magics, the seq_file implementation for why lseek lies about sizes on proc, the man pages for the errno contracts. The citations do the arguing.

It was reviewed, round after round, and merged upstream. By OstrichLab's own CEO.

The fix is the part of my internship application the reviewer suggested I delete.

07The light that lit

Fixes are promises until you run the old attack against them. So I did.

same rig, same attack, hardened build t+29s process killed by the runtime clean launch, hardened build: all green, decrypts normally

Twenty-nine seconds in, the runtime caught its own forged sky and pulled the floor out. Clean launches on the same build stayed green, so the kill was not the product being jumpy. It was the product being right.

I want to be precise about what that kill is. Every official light that should have recognized this work stayed dark. The QuartzLab eligibility filter never read the code. The OstrichLab advisory process declined the finding. The fix passed review. The finding did not. The scoring apparatus of this whole bout, the part that is supposed to light up when a touch lands, sat there like furniture.

Then the vendor shipped my defense, I ran my attack, and the product died on schedule. The only scoring lamp that ever lit for this touch is one I soldered into the opponent's weapon myself.

Nobody awarded it. The system confirmed me by refusing to run.

08The whole sport

This is the part that matters more than one product.

Every client-side self-defense system on the market, every mobile RASP, every DRM stack, every anti-cheat, runs in user space in the same process as its attacker. They differ in skill, not in predicament. The question "am I under attack?" has to be answered through channels that the attacker can interpose on, because there is nowhere else for user-space code to ask. Some hide the asking behind obfuscation, some rotate their tricks, some buy time. None of them change where the asking lives.

Detection in this architecture is best-effort, forever. What can be closed is the distance between detection and enforcement. If the sensor channel is kernel-direct and disagreement-aware, the cheap forgery stops working, and the attacker has to escalate to whole-system emulation. That is a real cost asymmetry: one interposition layer versus per-target, per-version emulation work.

09The ceiling

Honesty time, because a case file that only brags is a brochure.

The fix closed the proc-file channel and moved the high-value reads to the kernel. It did not close everything. Some property reads remain single-source. Directory enumeration still has interposable corners. The decrypted payload still exists in memory at handoff, and a patient attacker with the right privileges can go fishing for it.

And the true ceiling is architectural, not implementational. A sufficiently resourced attacker runs the entire app inside an emulator, feeds it a perfect world, and reads the plaintext at the class loader. No user-space defense escapes that, because the question is being asked inside the thing being emulated. The only walls that hold are the ones the emulator cannot reproduce: a TEE-held key secret, or a server that refuses to enroll a device it cannot attest. No mobile RASP on the market has built that wall. Until someone does, the honest phrase on every datasheet in this category is "raises the cost," and the honest reply is "of course it does. That is what a lock is."

Coda. OstrichLab is a stand-in. The real name is the Arabic word for astrolabe: the instrument that reads the sky to tell you where you stand. This one read the hand that held it. I know, because I was the hand. As for the bird, it earned its place the traditional way. The touch landed, and the head went into the sand.

The email from section 01 closes its loop here, on a clock. The note that the raw syscalls "add a lot of code without making detection stronger" arrived crossed with my Monday appeal. Tuesday night the bird's sensors learned to speak them. Friday morning the bird's CEO merged the branch. The advice and its refutation share one week on public tape. The merged fix disagrees, and so does the kill at second twenty-nine. The gate never opened. The bout happened anyway.

And the vendor's response, in full: the project is brand new with no active usage to qualify for a CVE. Early project or not, the foundation was wrong. Every sensor read through one hookable libc channel, and the key-derivation gates verified the forged state. That is not an early-stage rough edge. That is the load-bearing wall built wrong, whatever the version number says. Better models do not repair an assumption nobody examined.

The fix is public. The PoC stays held per coordinated disclosure. The receipts are on the tape.

No light. Touch registered anyway.
